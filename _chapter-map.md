# Chapter Map — US History with LLMs

*Built 2026-05-10 from OpenStax source under `chapters/[NN-slug]/`. Sections were extracted programmatically from the intro module of each folder; learning objectives were extracted from each section module. No source prose was rewritten — this map is a navigation document, not a draft.*

*Format per chapter: title, the 3–5 sections OpenStax actually delivers, the two or three core concepts the chapter genuinely turns on, the historical capabilities a student should leave with, key vocabulary worth teaching explicitly, and 3–4 Dig-Deeper candidates — concepts rich enough to reward a detour. The Dig-Deeper list will become the inline `↳ Dig Deeper` prompts during enrichment.*

## Chapter 1 — The Americas, Europe, and Africa Before 1492

**Sections:** The Americas · Europe on the Brink of Change · West Africa and the Role of Slavery
**Core concepts:** Three continents already had complex civilizations before contact; slavery existed in West Africa and Mediterranean Europe before the Atlantic system, but not in the same form; the technologies and incentives that drove European expansion (caravels, gunpowder, the Reconquista, the Silk Road's closure) were specific and recent.
**New capabilities:** Place pre-Columbian civilizations on a map without flattening them into "indigenous peoples"; distinguish chattel slavery from earlier slave systems; explain why Europe was the continent that crossed the Atlantic rather than China or West Africa.
**Vocabulary:** Mesoamerica, Mississippian, Reconquista, caravel, mercantilism (preview), trans-Saharan trade.
**Dig-deeper candidates:** Why the word *slave* derives from *Slav*; the engineering of Cahokia versus contemporaneous European cities; the role of the Black Death in reshaping European labor markets; the difference between West African slavery and Atlantic chattel slavery.

## Chapter 2 — Early Globalization: The Atlantic World, 1492–1650

**Sections:** Portuguese Exploration and Spanish Conquest · Religious Upheavals · Challenges to Spain's Supremacy · New Worlds: Labor, Commerce, and the Columbian Exchange
**Core concepts:** The Columbian Exchange as a biological and demographic event, not just a trade event; the Reformation as a destabilizer of Atlantic geopolitics; the *encomienda* and the early institutionalization of coerced labor.
**New capabilities:** Quantify the demographic catastrophe of the Americas after contact; trace how silver from Potosí funded European wars; explain how religious schism enabled (and constrained) colonial competition.
**Vocabulary:** Columbian Exchange, *encomienda*, *casta*, mercantilism, Treaty of Tordesillas.
**Dig-deeper candidates:** The 90 percent indigenous population collapse — what's known, what's estimated, what's disputed; how Potosí silver caused inflation across Europe and Asia; the *Valladolid debate* (Las Casas vs. Sepúlveda) and what it foreshadows.

---

## Chapter 3 — Creating New Social Orders: Colonial Societies, 1500–1700

**Sections:** Spanish Exploration and Colonial Society · Dutch and French Colonial Ambitions · English Settlements in America · The Impact of Colonization
**Core concepts:** Each European power built a different *kind* of colony (extractive, commercial, settlement); colonial social orders were engineered, not inherited; the shift from indentured to enslaved labor in the English colonies was contingent, not inevitable.
**New capabilities:** Distinguish a plantation society from a settler society from a trading-post empire; explain Bacon's Rebellion as a turning point in racialized labor; describe how Métis and other mixed communities reshaped the interior.
**Vocabulary:** *casta*, headright, indentured servitude, Bacon's Rebellion, Métis, *coureurs de bois*.
**Dig-deeper candidates:** The legal codification of hereditary racial slavery in Virginia (1662–1705); why New France stayed small; the Pueblo Revolt of 1680 as a successful indigenous uprising; comparing English New England with the Chesapeake.

## Chapter 4 — Rule Britannia! The English Empire, 1660–1763

**Sections:** Charles II and the Restoration Colonies · The Glorious Revolution and the English Empire · An Empire of Slavery and the Consumer Revolution · Great Awakening and Enlightenment · Wars for Empire
**Core concepts:** The British Empire as an integrated economic system, not a collection of separate colonies; the simultaneous rise of consumer culture and chattel slavery; the Great Awakening as the first transcolonial cultural event.
**New capabilities:** Trace a sugar-tobacco-slave triangle empirically; explain how Whig political theory traveled to the colonies; describe what the Seven Years' War actually was, in global terms, before it was an "American" story.
**Vocabulary:** Navigation Acts, salutary neglect, mercantilism (full), Great Awakening, Albany Plan.
**Dig-deeper candidates:** Why sugar mattered more than tobacco economically; the Bill of Rights (1689) and its colonial echoes; how George Whitefield's revival meetings worked as media events; the global scope of the Seven Years' War.

## Chapter 5 — Imperial Reforms and Colonial Protests, 1763–1774

**Sections:** Confronting the National Debt · The Stamp Act and the Sons and Daughters of Liberty · The Townshend Acts and Colonial Protest · The Destruction of the Tea and the Coercive Acts · The First Continental Congress
**Core concepts:** The Revolution began as a tax dispute over a war debt, not a movement for democracy; "no taxation without representation" was a specific constitutional claim about virtual vs. actual representation; the consumer boycott was the colonists' decisive weapon.
**New capabilities:** Distinguish Whig grievances from later Revolutionary ideology; explain how women's domestic labor (homespun cloth, tea substitutes) functioned as political protest; describe the Coercive Acts as a specific overreach.
**Vocabulary:** virtual representation, vice-admiralty courts, Sons/Daughters of Liberty, Committees of Correspondence, Intolerable/Coercive Acts.
**Dig-deeper candidates:** Why Parliament thought the colonists' position was incoherent; the role of *Common Sense* (preview); how the boycotts worked logistically; the difference between the First and Second Continental Congresses.

## Chapter 6 — America's War for Independence, 1775–1783

**Sections:** Britain's Law-and-Order Strategy · The Early Years of the Revolution · War in the South · Identity during the American Revolution
**Core concepts:** The Revolution was a civil war, not just an anti-colonial war (Loyalists, Patriots, the undecided, the enslaved choosing sides); the southern campaign was decided as much by enslaved people, partisans, and French intervention as by Continentals; the war ended in negotiated settlement, not unconditional victory.
**New capabilities:** Estimate Loyalist population fractions; describe Dunmore's Proclamation and its effects; explain why Yorktown ended the war (French fleet, southern exhaustion).
**Vocabulary:** Loyalist, Patriot, Hessian, Continental, Dunmore's Proclamation, Treaty of Paris (1783).
**Dig-deeper candidates:** Black Loyalists and their post-war exodus to Nova Scotia and Sierra Leone; the war from the British perspective; women's roles (camp followers, propagandists, irregulars); Indigenous nations choosing sides.

## Chapter 7 — Creating Republican Governments, 1776–1790

**Sections:** *Common Sense*: From Monarchy to Republic · How Much Revolutionary Change? · Debating Democracy · The Constitutional Convention and Federal Constitution
**Core concepts:** "Republicanism" meant something specific and contested in 1776 — virtuous citizens, mixed government, fear of corruption; the Articles of Confederation failed for traceable reasons; the Constitution was a compromise built around slavery in ways the text often obscures.
**New capabilities:** Parse the Three-Fifths Compromise as a specific power calculation; explain why the Articles couldn't tax; describe the Federalist/Anti-Federalist debate in their own terms.
**Vocabulary:** republicanism, virtue, mixed government, Articles of Confederation, Three-Fifths Compromise, Federalist Papers.
**Dig-deeper candidates:** Why Paine's *Common Sense* worked rhetorically; the contradictions of Jefferson's writing on slavery; Shays' Rebellion as catalyst; the Anti-Federalists' specific objections.

## Chapter 8 — Growing Pains: The New Republic, 1790–1820

**Sections:** Federalists and Democratic-Republicans · The New American Republic · Partisan Politics · The United States Goes Back to War
**Core concepts:** The first party system formed despite the founders' explicit opposition to parties; Hamilton's financial system was a real innovation with specific consequences; the War of 1812 was a near-disaster reframed in memory as a triumph.
**New capabilities:** Compare Hamilton's and Jefferson's economic visions concretely; explain the Alien and Sedition Acts as a free-speech precedent; describe the actual military outcomes of 1812 versus the political ones.
**Vocabulary:** assumption, Bank of the United States, Whiskey Rebellion, Alien and Sedition Acts, Hartford Convention.
**Dig-deeper candidates:** The XYZ Affair as a foreign-policy turning point; the Louisiana Purchase as a constitutional improvisation; the burning of Washington; **the timing wrinkle of the Battle of New Orleans** (becomes Chapter 00's hook).

## Chapter 9 — Industrial Transformation in the North, 1800–1850

**Sections:** Early Industrialization · A Vibrant Capitalist Republic · The Transportation Revolution · A New Social Order: Class Divisions
**Core concepts:** Industrialization was uneven, regional, and labor-specific (Lowell mill girls, putting-out system); the transportation revolution (canals, then railroads) restructured the economy; class became a visible category in American life for the first time.
**New capabilities:** Trace cotton from Mississippi plantation to Lowell mill to Liverpool market; explain why canals preceded railroads; describe the Panic of 1819 as a credit crisis.
**Vocabulary:** putting-out, Lowell system, internal improvements, market revolution, Panic of 1819.
**Dig-deeper candidates:** Why women's labor in textile mills was the prototype for industrial labor; the Erie Canal's specific economics; the difference between "panic" and "depression"; how internal improvements split the parties.

## Chapter 10 — Jacksonian Democracy, 1820–1840

**Sections:** From John Quincy Adams to Andrew Jackson · The Rise of American Democracy · The Nullification Crisis and the Bank War · Native American Removal · Tyranny and Triumph of the Majority
**Core concepts:** "Jacksonian democracy" expanded white-male suffrage while violently contracting Indigenous and Black political existence; the Bank War was a real fight over central banking with traceable consequences; nullification rehearsed Civil War arguments thirty years early.
**New capabilities:** Distinguish Jacksonian democracy from democratic theory generally; describe the Trail of Tears with specific casualty figures; explain how the Bank War caused the Panic of 1837.
**Vocabulary:** spoils system, nullification, Bank War, Trail of Tears, Indian Removal Act, *Worcester v. Georgia*.
**Dig-deeper candidates:** What Tocqueville actually saw versus what gets attributed to him; the *Worcester v. Georgia* ruling Jackson defied; the role of Martin Van Buren as party-builder; the simultaneous expansion and contraction of "the people."

## Chapter 11 — A Nation on the Move: Westward Expansion, 1800–1860

**Sections:** Lewis and Clark · The Missouri Crisis · Independence for Texas · The Mexican-American War · Free or Slave Soil?
**Core concepts:** Westward expansion was structurally about slavery extension (every new territory raised the question); "Manifest Destiny" was a coined phrase doing specific ideological work; the Mexican-American War was a war of choice that produced the territorial crisis of the 1850s.
**New capabilities:** Map the Missouri Compromise line; trace how Texas independence and annexation produced war; describe the Wilmot Proviso as a turning point.
**Vocabulary:** Manifest Destiny, Missouri Compromise, Wilmot Proviso, Mexican Cession, popular sovereignty.
**Dig-deeper candidates:** John L. O'Sullivan and the coinage of "Manifest Destiny"; the Bear Flag Republic; how Lincoln (then a congressman) opposed the Mexican War; the *Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo* and what happened to Mexican citizens left in U.S. territory.

## Chapter 13 — Antebellum Idealism and Reform Impulses, 1820–1860

**Sections:** An Awakening of Religion and Individualism · Antebellum Communal Experiments · Reforms to Human Health · Addressing Slavery · Women's Rights
**Core concepts:** The Second Great Awakening produced both abolitionism and proslavery theology; the reform impulse was tied to evangelical Protestantism in specific ways; women's rights and abolition were entangled movements from the start.
**New capabilities:** Connect Seneca Falls (1848) to abolitionist organizing; describe the temperance movement as a labor and gender movement; distinguish Garrisonian from political abolitionism.
**Vocabulary:** Second Great Awakening, perfectionism, Burned-Over District, Seneca Falls, abolitionism.
**Dig-deeper candidates:** Charles Grandison Finney's revival technique; the Oneida community as social experiment; the Grimké sisters' move from abolition to feminism; the *Declaration of Sentiments* compared to the *Declaration of Independence*.

## Chapter 14 — Troubled Times: The Tumultuous 1850s

**Sections:** The Compromise of 1850 · The Kansas-Nebraska Act and the Republican Party · The Dred Scott Decision · John Brown and the Election of 1860
**Core concepts:** Each "compromise" of the 1850s narrowed the political space for compromise; *Dred Scott* (1857) was a constitutional rupture, not a clarification; Lincoln's 1860 election under a four-candidate field with no Southern support made secession a question of when, not whether.
**New capabilities:** Parse the components of the 1850 Compromise individually; describe Bleeding Kansas as proto-civil-war; explain *Dred Scott*'s holding precisely (citizenship, territorial sovereignty, the Missouri Compromise).
**Vocabulary:** Fugitive Slave Act, popular sovereignty (in context), Bleeding Kansas, *Dred Scott v. Sandford*, Crittenden Compromise (preview).
**Dig-deeper candidates:** Why the Fugitive Slave Act radicalized Northern moderates; Roger Taney's reasoning in *Dred Scott*; the breakup of the Whig Party; the Lecompton constitution fight.

## Chapter 15 — The Civil War, 1860–1865

**Sections:** Origins and Outbreak · Early Mobilization and War · 1863: The Changing Nature of the War · The Union Triumphant
**Core concepts:** The war's causes are not contested among historians (slavery) but its memory has been contested for 160 years; the Emancipation Proclamation freed specific people in specific places (not "all enslaved people"); United States Colored Troops were decisive militarily.
**New capabilities:** State precisely what the Emancipation Proclamation did and did not do; explain why Antietam preceded the Proclamation; describe Sherman's March as targeted infrastructure destruction.
**Vocabulary:** secession, Anaconda Plan, Emancipation Proclamation, USCT, total war, Appomattox.
**Dig-deeper candidates:** **What the Emancipation Proclamation actually said** (a common LLM error site); the New York City draft riots; the role of foreign diplomacy in the war's outcome; the Confederate "Lost Cause" narrative as post-war invention.

## Chapter 16 — The Era of Reconstruction, 1865–1877

**Sections:** Restoring the Union · Congress and the Remaking of the South · Radical Reconstruction · The Collapse of Reconstruction
**Core concepts:** Reconstruction was the most ambitious experiment in multiracial democracy in American history, and it succeeded — for about a decade; the 13th, 14th, and 15th Amendments are a coherent set; Reconstruction's collapse was specific and traceable (Klan violence, Northern fatigue, the disputed 1876 election, the Compromise of 1877).
**New capabilities:** Distinguish Presidential from Congressional Reconstruction; describe the actual political achievements of Black Reconstruction governments; explain the Compromise of 1877 in detail.
**Vocabulary:** Black Codes, sharecropping, Reconstruction Amendments, Freedmen's Bureau, Ku Klux Klan, Redeemers.
**Dig-deeper candidates:** **The Compromise of 1877** (another LLM error site — often misstated); Hiram Revels and Blanche Bruce in the Senate; the Colfax Massacre; *United States v. Cruikshank*.

## Chapter 17 — Go West, Young Man: Westward Expansion, 1840–1900

**Sections:** The Westward Spirit · Homesteading: Dreams and Realities · Making a Living in Gold and Cattle · The Loss of American Indian Life and Culture · Chinese Immigrants and Hispanic Citizens
**Core concepts:** The post-Civil War West was a federally-subsidized project (railroads, Homestead Act, Indian wars); the destruction of the bison was deliberate policy; Chinese exclusion was the first federal racial exclusion act.
**New capabilities:** Map the transcontinental railroad routes and their land grants; describe the Dawes Act as cultural policy; explain the Chinese Exclusion Act (1882) and its lineage.
**Vocabulary:** Homestead Act, Dawes Act, transcontinental railroad, Wounded Knee, Chinese Exclusion Act.
**Dig-deeper candidates:** The Sand Creek Massacre and what made it different from prior violence; the Modoc War as test case; how the bison were destroyed and why; the *Ghost Dance* movement.

## Chapter 18 — Industrialization and the Rise of Big Business, 1870–1900

**Sections:** Inventors of the Age · From Invention to Industrial Growth · Building Industrial America on the Backs of Labor · A New American Consumer Culture
**Core concepts:** "Big business" was a new organizational form (the trust, the holding company) with specific legal innovations; American industrial growth happened on the backs of specific workers (immigrants, children, the formerly enslaved); the consumer culture of the late 19th century is the start of something still running.
**New capabilities:** Explain a trust structurally; describe the Haymarket affair and its consequences; distinguish craft from industrial unionism.
**Vocabulary:** trust, vertical/horizontal integration, scab, AFL, Knights of Labor, Haymarket.
**Dig-deeper candidates:** Carnegie's vertical integration of steel; the Pullman Strike and federal intervention; how Sears Roebuck reshaped rural America; the *Lochner v. New York* decision (preview).

## Chapter 19 — The Growing Pains of Urbanization, 1870–1900

**Sections:** Urbanization and Its Challenges · The African American "Great Migration" *[note: this is the late-19c. precursor; the named Great Migration is 1910s–]* and New European Immigration · Relief from the Chaos of Urban Life · Change Reflected in Thought and Writing
**Core concepts:** The American city of 1900 was structurally different from the city of 1870 (electric light, streetcars, tenements, the immigrant ward); ethnic neighborhoods were both refuges and traps; political machines provided services the state didn't.
**New capabilities:** Describe Tammany Hall as a service-delivery organization; distinguish "old" from "new" immigrants in the period's own terms (and critique that distinction); explain the settlement house movement.

## Chapter 20 — Politics in the Gilded Age, 1870–1900

**Sections:** Political Corruption in Postbellum America · Patronage, Tariffs, and Gold · Farmers Revolt in the Populist Era · Social and Labor Unrest in the 1890s
**Core concepts:** The Gilded Age party system was structurally weird — close national elections, regional party identification, low policy stakes; the gold-silver fight was real economic policy with real winners and losers; Populism almost reshaped American politics, then was absorbed.
**New capabilities:** Explain bimetallism in actual monetary terms; describe the People's Party platform; trace why William Jennings Bryan lost in 1896.
**Vocabulary:** bimetallism, free silver, Pendleton Act, civil service, Populist Party, Cross of Gold.
**Dig-deeper candidates:** Why Mark Twain coined "Gilded Age" as critique; the Pendleton Act's reform of the spoils system; the Omaha Platform; the *Cross of Gold* speech.

## Chapter 21 — Leading the Way: The Progressive Movement, 1890–1920

**Sections:** Origins of the Progressive Spirit · Progressivism at the Grassroots · New Voices for Women and African Americans · Progressivism in the White House
**Core concepts:** Progressivism was not one movement — it was a cluster (efficiency progressives, moral progressives, social-justice progressives) that frequently disagreed; it produced both the 19th Amendment and immigration restriction; it was both reformist and racially exclusionary.
**New capabilities:** Distinguish Booker T. Washington's accommodation from Du Bois's protest tradition; describe the *muckrakers* and name their work; explain the four Progressive Amendments (16, 17, 18, 19).
**Vocabulary:** muckraker, *initiative/referendum/recall*, NAACP, Niagara Movement, *progressivism*, Prohibition.
**Dig-deeper candidates:** *The Jungle* and the actual legislation it produced; Wilson's segregation of the federal civil service; Margaret Sanger and the contested history of family planning; the *Triangle Shirtwaist Factory fire*.

## Chapter 22 — Age of Empire: American Foreign Policy, 1890–1914

**Sections:** Turner, Mahan, and the Roots of Empire · The Spanish-American War · Economic Imperialism in East Asia · Roosevelt's Big Stick · Taft's Dollar Diplomacy
**Core concepts:** American empire was an explicit project with named theorists (Mahan on sea power, Turner on the frontier); the Spanish-American War acquired a Pacific empire almost by accident; the Open Door notes were a specific policy with specific aims.
**New capabilities:** Map the post-1898 American empire (Cuba, Puerto Rico, Philippines, Guam, Hawaii); describe the Philippine-American War (which kills more Americans than the war it followed); explain the Roosevelt Corollary to the Monroe Doctrine.
**Vocabulary:** Open Door Policy, Roosevelt Corollary, dollar diplomacy, Insular Cases, Spanish-American War.
**Dig-deeper candidates:** **The Philippine-American War** (often elided in U.S. textbooks); the *Insular Cases* and "unincorporated territories"; the annexation of Hawaii; the role of yellow journalism in 1898.

## Chapter 23 — Americans and the Great War, 1914–1919

**Sections:** American Isolationism and European Origins · The U.S. Prepares for War · A New Home Front · From War to Peace · Demobilization
**Core concepts:** American entry into WWI was politically engineered through specific moves (Wilson's "He Kept Us Out of War" reelection, then the Zimmermann telegram and unrestricted submarine warfare); the home front prosecuted dissent at a scale unmatched before or since; the Treaty of Versailles' rejection has long consequences.
**New capabilities:** Describe the Espionage and Sedition Acts and their use; explain the Fourteen Points versus the actual Treaty; trace the 1918–19 influenza pandemic alongside the war.
**Vocabulary:** Zimmermann telegram, Espionage Act, Fourteen Points, League of Nations, *Schenck v. United States*.
**Dig-deeper candidates:** The Great Migration (the *named* one, 1910s–40s) accelerated by wartime labor; the silencing of German-American culture; the Red Summer of 1919; the 1918 flu pandemic's American toll.

## Chapter 24 — The Jazz Age: Redefining the Nation, 1919–1929

**Sections:** Prosperity and the Production of Popular Entertainment · Transformation and Backlash · A New Generation · Republican Ascendancy
**Core concepts:** The 1920s prosperity was real and uneven — agriculture lagged, mining lagged, the South lagged; mass culture (radio, film, automobiles, jazz) was assembled in this decade; the decade's culture wars (Scopes, Prohibition, the second Klan, immigration restriction) prefigure later ones.
**New capabilities:** Describe the National Origins Act (1924) and its quotas; explain the Scopes trial as media event; map the Harlem Renaissance.
**Vocabulary:** flapper, Prohibition, Scopes Trial, Harlem Renaissance, National Origins Act, *the second Klan*.
**Dig-deeper candidates:** The 1921 Tulsa Race Massacre; the *Sacco and Vanzetti* case; how Hollywood became Hollywood; why Prohibition was constitutional rather than statutory.

## Chapter 25 — Brother, Can You Spare a Dime? The Great Depression, 1929–1932

**Sections:** The Stock Market Crash · Hoover's Response · The Depths of the Great Depression · Assessing the Hoover Years
**Core concepts:** The Crash was not the Depression; the banking failures of 1930–32 made the downturn catastrophic; Hoover did more than he is remembered for, and it was not enough.
**New capabilities:** Distinguish the 1929 crash from the 1930–33 banking crisis; describe the Reconstruction Finance Corporation as a Hoover-era precursor to New Deal institutions; explain the Bonus Army's significance.
**Vocabulary:** Black Tuesday, RFC, Smoot-Hawley, Bonus Army, Hooverville.
**Dig-deeper candidates:** The Dust Bowl as ecological history; why Smoot-Hawley made things worse; the role of the gold standard; Mexican Repatriation (often elided).

## Chapter 26 — Franklin Roosevelt and the New Deal, 1932–1941

**Sections:** The Rise of Franklin Roosevelt · The First New Deal · The Second New Deal
**Core concepts:** The New Deal was two distinct legislative bursts with different premises (recovery vs. structural reform); it built lasting institutions (Social Security, FDIC, SEC, NLRB) while leaving Black agricultural and domestic workers out by design; it ended depression less than the war did.
**New capabilities:** Distinguish First from Second New Deal legislation; describe the Social Security Act's exclusions and their racial geography; explain court-packing as a constitutional crisis.
**Vocabulary:** NIRA, AAA, WPA, CCC, Social Security Act, court-packing, Wagner Act.
**Dig-deeper candidates:** Why Social Security's exclusions made it pass; the Tennessee Valley Authority as regional planning experiment; Frances Perkins; *Schechter Poultry* and the end of NIRA.

## Chapter 27 — Fighting the Good Fight in World War II, 1941–1945

**Sections:** Origins of War: Europe, Asia, and the United States · The Home Front · Victory in the European Theater · The Pacific Theater and the Atomic Bomb

## Chapter 28 — Post-War Prosperity and Cold War Fears, 1945–1960

**Sections:** The Challenges of Peacetime · The Cold War · The American Dream · Popular Culture and Mass Media · The African American Struggle for Civil Rights
**Core concepts:** The Cold War was a specific construction (Truman Doctrine, NSC-68, containment) with American postwar prosperity built around it (GI Bill, defense spending, suburbanization); the suburb was federally subsidized and racially exclusive by policy (FHA redlining); the Civil Rights Movement's 1950s phase had its own logic (*Brown*, Montgomery, Little Rock).
**New capabilities:** Describe redlining as federal policy, not market emergence; explain the Marshall Plan as economic policy; map the early Civil Rights Movement's key cases and campaigns.
**Vocabulary:** containment, Truman Doctrine, Marshall Plan, GI Bill, redlining, McCarthyism, *Brown v. Board*.
**Dig-deeper candidates:** *Brown v. Board* implementation vs. the ruling; the Lavender Scare alongside McCarthyism; the Levittown model; the Korean War as the "forgotten" war.

## Chapter 31 — From Cold War to Culture Wars, 1980–2000

## Notes for enrichment

**Two reliable LLM-error sites identified by the map:**

1. **The Emancipation Proclamation** — LLMs frequently summarize it as "freed all enslaved people in the United States." It did not — it applied only to states still in rebellion as of January 1, 1863, and explicitly exempted the loyal border states and Union-occupied parts of the Confederacy. This is a clean test case for Chapter 15's Dig Deeper.

2. **The Battle of New Orleans / Treaty of Ghent timing** — LLMs typically describe the battle without noting it was fought after the peace treaty had been signed. Becomes Chapter 00's opening hook and Chapter 8's Dig Deeper.

3. **The Compromise of 1877** — Commonly oversimplified as a straight quid pro quo (presidency for the end of Reconstruction). The actual deal was murkier and partly informal. Chapter 16 Dig Deeper material.

4. **The Philippine-American War** — Frequently elided or named only briefly in LLM summaries of U.S. empire. Chapter 22 Dig Deeper.

5. **Social Security's racial exclusions** — LLMs describe the Social Security Act without noting it deliberately excluded agricultural and domestic workers (about 65% of Black workers). Chapter 26 material.

These five sites are good candidates to seed the LLM Exercise design for those chapters — a chapter where the running project lets the student catch an LLM error against a primary source is a chapter where the book's premise lands.
