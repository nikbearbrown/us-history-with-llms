## Premise

A US history textbook that teaches the history *and* teaches the student to use LLMs honestly while learning it. Two threads, braided. The history thread does the work of any good survey: primary sources, contested interpretations, the machinery of cause and consequence. The LLM thread runs alongside — what does Claude/ChatGPT/Gemini get right about Reconstruction, what does it confabulate about the Compromise of 1877, what happens when you ask three models the same question about the New Deal and they disagree.

The book treats LLMs the way a good chemistry textbook treats a balance: as an instrument with known precision, known failure modes, and earned trust. Not a friend. Not an oracle. Not a villain. An instrument.

## Voice notes

Inherits the workshop's Feynman register. History-specific adjustments:

- **Sources are the argument.** US history is a field where the question of *whose account* is part of the substance. The chapter that quotes Frederick Douglass on the Fourth of July is doing different work than the chapter that paraphrases him. Quote primary sources at length when the prose itself is the evidence.
- **Periodization is a choice, not a fact.** When the chapter says "the Gilded Age," it owes the reader a sentence on who drew the lines and what gets included or excluded by drawing them there.
- **The LLM thread is examined, not evangelized.** When a chapter asks the student to consult a model, it also asks them to verify, to compare against a primary source, to notice where the model's confidence outruns its evidence. The book's claim is that LLMs are useful *and* that learning history requires learning when not to trust them.
- **Contested interpretations stay contested.** The Civil War's causes, the New Deal's effects, the Cold War's necessity — these are live disputes among historians. The book names the disputes, names the historians, and hands the student the evidence to form their own reading. The author has views; the views are labeled "I" and the reasoning is shown.
